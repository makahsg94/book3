
===== 1A :: narrative tenses :: p9
0: When we tell a story, we often use a variety of tenses. These are called ‘narrative tenses’ and they include the past simple, the past continuous and the past perfect. It is important to use the correct tense as this gives us information about the sequence of events and the most important details of the story.
1: It was a cold, wet day and the wind was blowing. Josh was walking down the road when suddenly, he heard a terrible crash. He turned around and saw that a woman had crashed her moped.
2: Luckily, the woman was fine, but the moped had been badly damaged.
3: ## Past simple
4: We use the past simple to talk about the main events of a story and other completed actions.
5: Suddenly, he heard a terrible crash.
6: She woke up early and decided to go out for a walk.
7: ## Past continuous
8: We use the past continuous:
9: • to give background information to a story.
10: • to refer to temporary or changing past states
11: and situations.
12: The sun was shining. The wind was blowing. (background information)
13: He was living in Brazil. (at the time of the story, temporary)
14: We were travelling around Australia. (temporary)
15: ## Past perfect
16: We use the past perfect to talk about an action which happened before the main events.
17: When I got to the top floor office I realised I left my phone in the car.
18: When we arrived at the station, the train had already left.
19: The past perfect links a past point (we arrived at the station) to a point further back in time (the train had left).

===== 1B :: verb patterns :: p12
0: Sometimes we put two verbs together.
1: They hope to learn Spanish.
2: I like playing ball games.
3: When the first verb takes a preposition (except to ), the second verb is usually in the - ing form.
4: You should think about doing that course.
5: I finally succeeded in finding a job.
6: We believe in telling the truth.
7: You should concentrate on passing your exams.
8: I dream about playing for that team.
9: She apologised for getting angry.
10: He struggles with working at night.
11: I care about saving the environment.
12: When a phrasal verb is followed by a verb, the second verb is usually in the - ing form.
13: We carried on driving for another hour.
14: I look forward to reading your new book!
15: She gave up studying at night.
16: We put off making that decision.
17: Some phrasal verbs can be followed by an infinitive or an -ing form, but with a change in meaning.
18: Compare the two sentences.
19: She went on dancing even after her accident.
20: She went on to be a great dancer.
21: Go on + - ing means ‘continue’.
22: Go on + to infinitive means ‘end up’.
23: He grew up watching his father play football.
24: He grew up to be a great footballer.
25: Grow up + - ing means ‘do something while changing from being a child to an adult’.
26: Grow up + to infinitive describes what a person becomes as an adult.
27: Some verbs (without prepositions) can be followed by a to infinitive or an -ing form, but with a change in meaning.
28: I stopped to talk to Felipe.
29: I stopped listening to rock music years ago.
30: Stop + to infinitive means ‘paused an action in order to do a different action’.
31: Stop + - ing means ‘change a habit or finish a repeated/extended action’.
32: I must remember to set the alarm.
33: I remember playing with those toys when I was five or six.
34: Remember + to infinitive means you have a responsibility which you must not forget.
35: Remember + - ing means you have a memory of something that happened in the past.

===== 1D :: except for, apart from, (not) even :: p16
0: except for We use except for + noun to say that something is not included.
1: The school teaches most ball sports except for golf.
2: We use except for to introduce the one person/thing that means a statement isn’t 100 percent true.
3: All the children are here except for Leila.
4: (Leila is the only child who isn’t here.) I like vegetables, except for onions.
5: (Onions are the only vegetables I don’t like.) When except for follows a noun, we can omit for with no change in meaning.
6: She loves ball games except (for) golf.
7: They speak all the Latin-based languages except (for) Romanian.
8: When we use except as a conjunction that joins two clauses, it cannot be followed by for. The meaning is the same as except for.
9: My dogs are similar except for the oldest one is bigger.
10: The books look good except for the covers are slightly damaged.
11: apart from Apart from + noun means the same as except for.
12: We’re open every day apart from Sunday.
13: Apart from Kenzo, the whole family came to the wedding.
14: I like all types of films apart from documentaries.
15: Apart from the sound of the clocks, the house was silent.
16: even We use even to emphasise that something is surprising or unusual.
17: John doesn’t usually watch TV, but even he liked that programme!
18: I’ll help you with the project. I’ll even stay with you after work to finish it.
19: not even We use not even to emphasise a surprising negative point.
20: Not even Jo, whose English is very good, could understand what he was saying.
21: She’d changed so much I didn’t even recognise her!

===== 2A :: present perfect continuous :: p22
0: We use the present perfect continuous to talk about actions and situations which started in the past, but are not finished. They continue until now.
1: I’ve been studying Mandarin since last year.
2: (I’m still studying Mandarin.) He’s been living in the UK for ten years. (He still lives in the UK.) We use the past simple when the action or situation is finished.
3: I studied Mandarin last year. (I’m not studying it now.) He lived in the UK for ten years. (He’s not living there now.) We form the present perfect continuous with been + -ing form.
4: We use the present perfect continuous with for and since to talk about the length or duration of an activity, or when it started.
5: We use since to refer to a point in time.
6: They’ve been living in New Zealand since 2020.
7: She’s been waiting for you since 2 p.m.
8: We use for to talk about a period of time.
9: We’ve been working together for over twenty years.
10: We use How long …? to ask about the length of time.
11: A: How long have you been playing the drums?
12: B: I’ve been playing since I was six years old.
13: We use the present perfect continuous with recently, lately, etc. to talk about actions and situations that are relevant now.
14: I haven’t been sleeping very well recently.
15: She’s been working very hard lately. (She deserves a promotion.)
16: We often use the present perfect simple or the present perfect continuous (with little change in meaning) especially for verbs such as live, work, teach, I’ve lived here for years.
17: I’ve been living here for years.
18: We do NOT use the present perfect continuous with state verbs (e.g. love, hate, like, have, enjoy, know, understand believe, etc.). With these verbs we use the present perfect simple.
19: I’ve known him for ages. NOT I’ve been knowing him for ages.
20: I’ve had this car for three years. NOT I’ve been having this car for three years.

===== 2B :: relative clauses :: p24
0: We use defining relative clauses to give essential information about a person, a thing, a place, a time or a possessive.
1: We use relative pronouns who, which, where, when and whose to introduce the relative clause.
2: A specialist is someone who knows a lot about a subject.
3: Do you remember the time when we went on holiday together?
4: Once there was a girl whose name was Lilibet. In defining relative clauses we can use that to replace who, which or when.
5: Swimming is something that always makes me feel better.
6: We can leave out who, which and that when the relative clause already has a subject.
7: Rob Jenkins was a teacher ( who ) I knew at college.
8: I is the subject of the verb knew. Who is the object of the verb, so can be left out.
9: Rob Jenkins was a teacher who taught me maths.
10: Who is the subject of the verb taught, so we must keep who.
11: We can replace a defining relative clause with verb + -ing.
12: The winner is the person ending up with the most points. = The winner is the person who ends up with the most points.
13: We can replace a defining relative clause with infinitive. We often use to + infinitive with the first, the last, the only and superlative adjectives.
14: Who was the last person to see John? = Who was the last person who saw John?

===== 2C :: How to … talk about things that annoy you :: p27
0: We use these phrases to talk about things that annoy us.
1: I can’t stand it.
2: I can’t bear it.
3: It really annoys me.
4: It gets on my nerves.
5: It bugs me.
6: It drives me crazy/nuts/mad.
7: Don’t get me started on (people who never say thank you).

10: I can’t bear it when that happens.
11: It gets on my nerves when people drop litter.
12: It drives me nuts when people don’t answer my messages.
13: We can also use a relative clause or -ing.
14: Children that misbehave in restaurants really bug me.
15: I can’t stand people shortening my name.
16: We can use other short phrases to agree or disagree.
17: Yes, so annoying.
18: I don’t mind that.
19: That doesn’t bother me.
20: ## Verbs in the continuous form
21: We can use the present and past continuous with always, constantly, continually, forever to describe a habit which we find annoying.
22: You’re always losing your phone!
23: My dad’s forever complaining about noise.
24: Our car was continually breaking down.
25: We also sometimes use this structure to show a positive emotion.
26: Children at this age are so cute. They’re constantly laughing!

===== 3A :: conditional structures: unless, even if, in case (of) :: p34
0: We use unless to mean if not.
1: Unless you hurry up, we’re going to leave without you.
2: (If you don’t hurry up, we’re going to leave without you.)
3: Please don’t park here unless you’re a member of staff. (Please don’t park here if you’re not a member of staff.)
4: When the unless clause is first in a sentence, we use a comma at the end of the clause.
5: Unless she speaks slowly, I can’t understand Liz.
6: When the unless clause is second, we don’t usually use a comma before it.
7: I can’t understand Liz unless she speaks slowly.
8: ## even if
9: We use even if for emphasis, to say that something will not change a situation.
10: Even if the company pays me twice as much, I’m going to leave.
11: I’ll never speak to her again even if she says sorry.
12: When the main clause and the even if clauses are long, we use a comma to divide them.
13: ## in case
14: We use in case to talk about being prepared for the possibility that something might happen.
15: Take your umbrella in case it rains.
16: We often use it with just.
17: I’ll make some sandwiches just in case we get hungry later.
18: The in case clause is usually second in a sentence.
19: We don’t use commas before or after the clause.
20: ## in case of
21: We use in case of + noun in formal situations to mean ‘if something happens’. It is often about a bad or dangerous situation.
22: In case of fire, break the glass.
23: In case of bad weather, the ceremony will be held indoors.
24: We often use it in formal written information.
25: The in case of clause usually comes first in a sentence.
26: We use unless, even if and in case + present tenses to speak about the present or the future.
27: I can’t help you unless you tell me the problem.
28: NOT I can’t help you unless you’ll tell me the problem.
29: We can also use unless, even if and in case + past tenses when we are speaking more hypothetically.
30: Even if I won a million euros, I wouldn’t change my job.

===== 3B :: necessity, obligation and permission :: p36
0: necessity, obligation and permission
1: ## Necessity and obligation
2: Many phrases can express both necessity and obligation, depending on the context.
3: I need to take some time off. (It’s important that I take time off. It’s a necessity.) I need to finish this report by tomorrow.
4: (Someone, for example my manager, expects me to finish the report. It’s an obligation.) We use be necessary to, be essential to and needs to be done when the focus is on the action.
5: We use be necessary to + infinitive to say that you must do or be something.
6: It’s necessary to have a driving licence. ([You] must have a driving licence.) We use be essential to + infinitive to say that something is necessary and important.
7: It’s essential to take her to hospital immediately.
8: ([We] must take her to hospital immediately. It’s very important.) We can also use need to + be + past participle to say something is an obligation or a necessity.
9: This job needs to be done by tomorrow morning.
10: We use have got to and be supposed to when the focus is on the person.
11: We use have got to + infinitive for obligation and necessity. It means must or have to.
12: It is a little more informal than must or have to.
13: I’ve got to get home by ten.
14: In informal spoken English this is often pronounced gotta.
15: She’s gotta think about it.
16: We use be supposed to + infinitive for something which is an obligation, but which we don’t always do.
17: I’m supposed to wear a hat when I deliver food, but I don’t.
18: Robbie’s not supposed to call customers by their first names, but he often does.
19: ## Permission
20: We use be (not) allowed to + infinitive to say that something is OK, you can do it, or it’s not OK, you can’t do it.
21: Our children are allowed to play computer games for two hours at the weekends.
22: I’m not allowed to go to bed after midnight.
23: We can also use be (not) permitted to to mean the same thing. It is more formal than be allowed. Office workers here are permitted to wear casual clothes on Fridays.
24: Drivers are not permitted to drive more than eight hours without a break.

===== 3D :: expressing preferences :: p40
0: ## would rather
1: We use would rather + infinitive + than to talk about:
2: • our general preferences.
3: Zayan would rather cook with electricity than gas.
4: • specific situations.
5: I’d rather pay by phone if that’s all right.
6: • hypothetical situations.
7: I’d rather be happy than (be) rich.
8: Would you rather spend a night in an aquarium or a library?
9: To make the negative, we use would/’d rather not + infinitive.
10: I’d rather not answer that question.
11: We use would much / far rather to show a strong preference.
12: We’d much rather go skiing than surfing.
13: I’d far rather cycle to work than go by underground.
14: ## prefer, would prefer
15: We use prefer + noun/- ing form + to to talk about:
16: • our general preferences.
17: I prefer cats to dogs.
18: In general he prefers working alone to working with other people.
19: We use would prefer + to + infinitive to talk about:
20: • specific situations.
21: I’d prefer to get a takeaway tonight.
22: • hypothetical situations.
23: Would you prefer to live in the Arctic or the Sahara Desert?
24: To make the negative, we use would/’d prefer not to + infinitive.
25: I’d prefer not to meet today. Tomorrow is better.
26: We use much prefer and definitely prefer to show a strong preference.
27: I much prefer playing tennis to watching it on TV.
28: I definitely prefer cats to dogs.
29: I’d much/definitely prefer to go by train.
30: We can also use would go for to mean would choose/prefer.
31: Which would you rather be, a teacher or a doctor?
32: I’d go for a teacher.

===== 4A :: past plans and intentions :: p45
0: We use was/were going to to talk about a planned action in the past that didn’t happen.
1: I was going to call you, but I was too busy.
2: They were going to visit the museum, but it was closed.
3: The negative is wasn’t/weren’t going to.
4: We can also use planned to and hoped to as alternatives. These have a similar meaning to going to. We can use these in the past simple or past continuous.
5: I planned to visit last week, but I couldn’t in the end.
6: I was planning to take you to the new café, but we didn’t have time.
7: I hoped to finish my project yesterday, but something went wrong.
8: I was hoping to meet your father, but he wasn’t there.
9: The negative of planned to is wasn’t planning to. I wasn’t planning to go anywhere this summer.
10: (But maybe this changed.) We can use other verbs in the past continuous to describe past intentions: I was expecting to go to Turkey that year.
11: They were waiting to finish university before they got married.
12: We can also use meant to to talk about a planned action in the past that didn’t happen. We use it more in the simple than the continuous.
13: I meant to introduce you to Xavier, but he left. We meant to invite you, but we couldn’t find your phone number.
14: The negative is didn’t mean to. We usually use this to describe when someone does something by accident.
15: Sorry – I crashed the car! I didn’t mean to!
16: She didn’t mean to cause trouble. She was trying to help.
17: We use intend to to show a planned action in the past. This sounds more formal than meant to, planned to, etc. We use it more in the simple than the continuous.
18: The doctor intended to see me last week, but he had to cancel.
19: We often use intend to focus on the reasons for an action.
20: He bought the ring because he intended to ask her to marry him.
21: The negative is didn’t intend.
22: She didn’t intend to do it. It was an accident.
23: We use thought it would be to talk about the reason for a planned action in the past.
24: I brought you because I thought it would be nice if you met my parents.

===== 4B :: indirect and negative questions :: p47
0: indirect and negative questions
1: ## Direct questions
2: To make direct questions we usually use the word order: (question word) + auxiliary verb + subject + main verb (infinitive).
3: question auxiliary subject main verb word verb (infinitive) + phrase What do you know about the situation in …?
4: Where does he work?
5: Where should we put these bags?
6: ## Indirect questions
7: We use indirect questions to sound more polite (and less direct). After the opening phrase ( Could you tell me …? Can I ask you …? Do you know …?
8: we use the affirmative form.
9: Direct: Where do I have to go?
10: Indirect: Could you tell me where I have to go NOT Could you tell me where do I have to go?
11: ## Negative questions
12: We can also use negative questions to sound polite.
13: For negative questions we use the negative form instead of the affirmative form.
14: Wouldn’t you like a drink/something to eat?
15: Don’t you want to take your coat off?
16: Instead of: Would you like a drink/something to eat?
17: Do you want to take your coat off?
18: We use negative questions when we want to check or confirm information (we think we know the answer). We can also use negative questions to show surprise.
19: Didn’t she win an award? (I think she won an award, so I’m checking the information/confirming my belief.) Isn’t it time to go? (I think it’s time to go, and I’m surprised that you are not ready yet.) Did you see that documentary? (genuine question – I don’t know the answer.) Didn’t you see that documentary? (I expected the answer to be yes, and by using a negative question I show my surprise that I now think you didn’t.) We can use negative questions to try to persuade people that our idea is the best.
20: Don’t you think that we should take a taxi?
21: (I think that we should take a taxi.) Wouldn’t you say that this film is more interesting? (I think this film is more interesting.) Negative questions can be confusing to answer. To help with this, we can answer a negative question with a short sentence instead of yes/no.
22: A: Wouldn’t you like a drink?
23: B: Thank you, that would be lovely.

===== 5A :: clauses of purpose: to, so as to, in order to/that, so that :: p57
0: clauses of purpose: to, so as to Clauses of purpose with to infinitive We use clauses of purpose to talk about our reason for doing something.
1: (I’m organising a party) (to celebrate my birthday.) the action the purpose We use to + infinitive and in order to + infinitive to introduce purpose clauses.
2: I’m going downstairs to lock up.
3: I often turn off my phone in order to avoid distractions. We can also use so as to + infinitive. We often use this for more formal situations.
4: The police put up barriers so as to stop people entering the building.
5: Notice The subject of the main clause and the clause of purpose is always the same.
6: ( She is studying English) ( in order to get a better job).
7: She is studying and she wants to get a better job.
8: ## Clauses of purpose with that
9: We can use so that + subject + verb or in order that + verb to talk about purpose.
10: We use these when the subject of the two clauses is the same or different.
11: I’d like to move closer to the stage so that I can hear. The school is changing the exam date in order that we have more time to prepare.
12: Remember: when the subject of the two clauses is different, we use so that or in order that but NOT in order to I’m going to send you all the information so that you can make a decision. NOT … in order to make a decision Clauses of purpose in the negative We put not before to in negative clauses of purpose: not to, so as not to.
13: He set his alarm for 7 a.m. in order not to oversleep. I check my ‘to do’ list every day so as not to forget. We can use not to, but it is not common except as a contrast.
14: I eat a lot of vegetables, not to lose weight but for my health.
15: We use a normal negative after so that and in order that Let’s check this very carefully so that we don’t make a mistake.
16: The company gave us our money back in order that we wouldn’t post a negative review.
17: ## Clause order
18: The clauses of purpose usually come after the main clause, but they can come first.
19: When they come first, they are followed by a comma.
20: In order to buy a car, I had to save up for a year.
21: So that the team could take a break, all matches in August were cancelled.

===== 5B :: comparative and superlative structures :: p61
0: comparative and superlative structures
1: ## Comparatives
2: We use comparative adjectives to compare nouns, and comparative adverbs to compare most verbs.
3: My new company is busier than my old one.
4: Could you speak more quietly, please?
5: We use a comparative adjective with verbs of the senses and feelings (e.g. look, taste, smell, feel, sound, seem ).
6: It seems quieter here than I remember.
7: We often use (not) as + adjective/adverb + as My chair isn’t as comfortable as yours.
8: I don’t speak Spanish as well as you do.
9: We use quantifiers (a bit, a little, slightly, much, a lot) with comparative adjectives and adverbs to talk about how much difference there is.
10: This desk is slightly more expensive than that one, but it’s better value. Dimitris is writing a lot more confidently now than he used to.
11: ## Superlatives
12: We use superlative adjectives and adverbs to compare something with the rest of the group.
13: This is the least expensive way to send the package.
14: Mateo and Luciana have travelled the furthest. We use quantifiers ( much, by far ) with superlative adjectives and adverbs to talk about how much difference there is.
15: Hyun Woo is by far the most talented young actor I’ve ever seen.
16: She talked much more clearly than I’d expected.
17: ## Emphasising
18: We can repeat a comparative adjective or adverb to emphasise change.
19: The situation is more and more dangerous every day.
20: As we left the city, Bianca drove faster and faster. We use than ever with comparative adjectives and adverbs for emphasis.
21: The new series is better than ever.
22: Hillary is working harder than ever.
23: It means the same as than before, but gives more emphasis. We can also use ever or ever before.
24: We’ve had more forest fires this year than before/ever/ever before. We also use ever with superlative adjectives.
25: We’ve built a lot of hotels, but this is the tallest ever. Other structures with comparatives and superlatives We use parallel phrases with the + comparative adjective/adverb, + comparative adjective/adverb.
26: The bigger the car, the more expensive it is, usually.
27: The more you practise, the better you’ll become.
28: Notice The comparative begins each clause.
29: NOT you practise the more, you’ll get the better.
30: We often use one of, some of, among with superlative adjectives.
31: This model is among our most expensive cars.

===== 5C :: How to … summarise information from different sources :: p63
0: We use these phrases to summarise information that we read or hear.
1: saying where we got an idea from From what X say(s), …
2: From what everyone says, it’s a very reliable company.
3: According to X, …
4: According to the reviews, the film is the best ever in the series.

===== 6A :: so and such :: p69
0: have similar meanings. We can use so + adjective/adverb to show emphasis or strong feelings about something. Without so, the sentence would still be complete.
1: That part of the city is beautiful. ➞ That part of the city is so beautiful!
2: She played the guitar brilliantly. ➞ She played the guitar so brilliantly!
3: We can also use so + adjective/adverb + that to describe something that leads to a result. In this use, without so, the sentence would not be complete.
4: It was so cold that we needed coats. NOT It was cold that we needed coats.
5: The restaurant was so busy that we couldn’t get a table.
6: NOT The restaurant was busy that we couldn’t get a table.
7: We can use such + a/an (+ adjective) + singular noun to show emphasis or strong feelings about something. An adjective often comes before the noun. Without such, the sentence would still be complete.
8: We had such a nice day walking around the city.
9: The hotel was such a long way from the station.
10: It’s such a wealthy area.
11: The house was such a mess!
12: We can use such + uncountable or plural nouns.
13: It’s such bad weather.
14: The café serves such good coffee.
15: They’re such friendly people.
16: We had such fun.
17: We can also use such + (a/an) + adjective + noun + clause to describe something that leads to a result. In this use, without such, the sentence would not be complete.
18: They had such a good time that they went back the next day. NOT They had a good time that they went back the next day.
19: It was such a boring job that she decided to change companies. NOT It was a boring job that she decided to change companies.
20: I had such heavy bags that I couldn’t walk very far.
21: NOT I had heavy bags that I couldn’t walk very far.
22: He gave us such good advice that we had no problems at all. NOT He gave us good advice that we had no problems at all.
23: In spoken and informal language we sometimes omit that from so/such sentences with a clause.
24: I was so tired ( that ) I fell asleep.
25: It was such a boring film ( that ) we left before the end.

===== 6B :: be/get used to :: p72
0: We use be used to + noun/- ing to talk about something you are accustomed to doing. It is usual for you to do this.
1: I’m a pilot, so I’m used to flying.
2: The weather is hot, but we live in southern Spain, so we are used to it. The negative is be not used to.
3: I’m not used to working at weekends.
4: We use get used to + noun/- ing to talk about something you become accustomed to. It is no longer unusual or strange.
5: At first I didn’t like the food, but I soon got used to it. I don’t think I’ll ever get used to their accent. It’s difficult to understand.
6: The negative is not get used to.
7: We never got used to the pollution in the city.
8: Notice be/get used to are different from used to We use used to + infinitive to talk about a habit or state in the past, which is usually something you don’t do now or is no longer the case.
9: We used to live on a boat. Now we live in a house.
10: I didn’t use to like climbing. Now I love it.

===== 7A :: reported speech :: p81
0: When we report what someone said earlier, we use a past reporting verb.
1: We also often make changes to the original sentence.
2: ‘I’m in a hurry.’ Feisal said that he was in a hurry.
3: Reporting verbs include agreed, answered, complained, explained, promised, realised, replied, said, told.
4: Tense and pronoun changes: revision After a reporting verb, the original verb often moves one tense back into the past.
5: We change the personal pronouns and adjectives (e.g.
6: his, she, her, etc.)
7: direct speech → reported speech: present simple → past simple. ‘I speak Spanish.’ Heidi told me that she spoke Spanish.

9: past simple and present perfect → past perfect: ‘We went to the same school.’ Alan said that they had gone to the same school.

15: We agreed that the dinner had been terrible.

19: ‘I’m on my way to meet you.’ Han says he’s on his way to meet us.

===== 7B :: passives :: p85
0: We use the passive to focus on the person or thing which is affected by an action rather than the person or thing doing the action.
1: The app has been downloaded over a million times.

3: • is obvious.

5: • is unknown.

===== 8A :: third conditional and should have :: p94
0: third conditional and should have
1: ## Third conditional
2: We use the third conditional to talk about imaginary or hypothetical events in the past.
3: These are unreal or impossible situations. We often use the third conditional to talk about regrets (things we’d like to be different about the past).
4: conditional clause result clause If + past perfect, would + have + past participle If I had woken up on time, I wouldn’t have missed the bus. (It’s impossible to change this because the situation has already happened.) We would have left early if we’d known about the terrible traffic. (We didn’t know about the traffic, so we didn’t leave early.) We can use could have + past participle or might have + past participle instead of would have to talk about alternative actions in the past. We use could have and might have when we are less certain of the result of something.
5: Maybe we could have won if we’d played better.
6: If I’d known she was at the party, I might have come.
7: should/shouldn’t have We use should/shouldn’t have to talk about regrets (things we’d like to be different about the past).
8: I should have bought my ticket yesterday.
9: Now there are none left.
10: I shouldn’t have eaten so much. Now I feel ill.

===== 8B :: would :: p96
0: We can use would to talk about past habits.
1: We can also use the past simple.
2: My father would read to me every night. (habit) My father read to me every night.
3: We don’t use would to talk about past states.
4: As a child, I was very curious. NOT As a child, I would be very curious.
5: ## used to
6: We can use used to when talking about past habits or states, which have often changed or are not true now. We can also use the past simple.
7: As a child, I used to love reading. (habit) As a child, I loved reading.
8: When we lived in New Zealand, I used to feel so isolated from my family. (state) When we lived in New Zealand, I felt so isolated from my family.
9: When we use an adverb of frequency ( often, always, sometimes, etc.) with used to or would the word order is different.
10: When we use would it goes before the adverb of frequency.
11: We would always see them in the afternoon.
12: We would often visit my grandparents during the holidays.
13: When we use used to it goes after the adverb of frequency.
14: We always used to see them in the afternoon.
15: We often used to visit my grandparents during the holidays.
